Urban trees and summer heat

As cities grow, roads and buildings replace soil and vegetation. These hard surfaces absorb heat during the day and release it slowly at night. As a result, urban areas can remain warmer than nearby rural areas, a phenomenon known as the urban heat island effect.

Trees can reduce local temperatures in two ways. Their leaves provide shade, preventing sunlight from heating surfaces directly. They also release water vapour through a process called transpiration, which cools the surrounding air. However, the size of the benefit depends on the species, the availability of water and the arrangement of buildings.

A study in one city found that shaded streets were cooler on summer afternoons than comparable streets without trees. The researchers cautioned that their measurements were taken during a single summer and did not establish how much energy residents saved. They recommended combining tree planting with other measures, including lighter-coloured roofs.
